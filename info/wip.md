## Background

### Levant Turmoil
Mustafa Iodinm had long stated that he had intention to build a strong Federation, cooptating neighboring colonies as members, such as Iraq and the Levant region. When the Federation won the turk civil war and the Federation was born in 1923, sympathy grew among the populations under French and British Mandate, that were part of the previous Ottoman Empire, specially among Arab ethnic group and Islamics.
The success of the separation of the Turkish and Kurdish states, as well as smaller independente city-states like Izmir and Istambul, under a strong centralized flag that could not easily be shaken by external pressure, inspired hope in the Arab and Kurd populations in and around the Middle East.
The first united group to be opening vocal about the Syria (and later Lebanon) joining the federation was the Levant Liberation Front, under leadership of Iyad Ibrahim. This eventually escalated to the Levant Uprising, in 4 September 1931, in Damascus and Beirut. Despite initial intention for intervention by the MEF in figures like Iodinm and the Kurd governor Sümer Ünal (create this page, this was the Turk state governor at the time), the Federation was still in the process of rapid industrialization and the army Chief of the General Staff Mehmet Günaltay was against a large scale operation as the army was still under reorganization and modernization. The intervention was eventually rulled ou in the Council, specially because the bulk of the revolt was far south of the border. The uprising was supressed in 20 September, with Ibrahim sentenced to death. Iodinm published statements against the colonial powers that spreaded across the Levant and called out for their freedom, stating that the peoples will should be followed.
The question was never settled and insatisfaction grew strong in the region. In 1936 more rebel groups had formed and the MEF was ready and willing to take action. The failing by the French in signing the Franco-Syrian Treaty in September 1936 eventually led to the intervention. 

### MEF Preparations
Iodinm knew the great powers had industrial and technological advantage for years, and those differences were clearly underlined in the World War I. He and the MEF high command understood war that was not only about numbers, but also about supply, logistics, and large scale production, all of which the Federation was lacking on its birth in 1923. In the following years, the industrial base and logistic chain was greatly expanded, with multiple railroads built, roads, and factories. Iodinm seeked Germand and Soviet expertise and machinery, and incentivised external capital (specially from Arab nations) to invest in the Federation. Multiple Federal factories were built, owned by the central government but operated regionally.
The military industry was not prioritized until later in 1935 when the military high command received a proper R&D department, but the MEF army was highly reliant in foreign equipment well into 1936.
The MEF high command was already receiving limited German military counseling since the first years of the MEF birth at the end of the turkish civil war. With Hitler's rise to power, this intensified, as a Hitler saw the newly formed nation as threat to British and French mandates and oil sources. Germany built and tested weapons in Federation soil and brough advisors like Max Bauer and Hans von Seeckt that highlighted the spearhead and logistic supply chain. In this way, German structure of command and doctrines was highly influentional to the MEF high command and training.

## Prelude
When tensions rose in the starting years of 1936 and the Federal Council decided the MEF would not abstain again from taking action, the High Command started to draft plans for the war. It was believed that the French would not initially believe the MEF would take military action and risk a open war with a much stronger opponent, and if it did, they would have more than enough time to counter act it, so they didn't believe French will field a big defensive army (which was proven correct). It was also known that France had bigger concerns in Europe, as the Remilitarization of the Rhineland in March raised alarms in the Allies. Because of that, the initial days were considered essential to estabilish a offensive position.
Mehmet Günaltay proposed that Aleppo and Latakia where taken as primary objectives, depriving the French from an important logistic center and port, while a second, smaller offensive would seek to advance towards Raqqa and the Euphrates River and estabilish a defensive line across the river banks. The subsequent invasion would be concentrated on the west side of Syria, moving south towards Beirut and Damascus, and causing a war of attrition that would eventually put the French public against the war. Although some of the terrain in the west could present challenges, the flatter desert to the east was considered too be a big logistical hassle.
General Cemil Toydemir proposed a similar but more ambicious plan. He knew the French army was bigger and could field many divisions, and keep them supplied much longer than the MEF could. Instead of wasting time in a urban warfare in Aleppo, the bulk of the army would press on both sides of the city, with the eastern group swifting west and creating a hook around Aleppo and towards Latakia. This plan was a scaled down version of the German Schlieffen Plan. Toydemir believed that if the action was successful, and if the offensive towards Raqqa was conducted at the same time, the French would not be able to reinforce their rear, leaving the south region open for advance towards Beirut. With the port of Beirut and the Lebanese coast taken, the smaller force the French army had deployed in the Levant would be trapped and beyond any hope of being reinforced by sea.
This plan carried many risks though. If the French decided to abandon the east and reinforce the Hama region, the MEF army could be caught marchin and unprepared for fighting. Even worse, if France brought in reinforcements in the port of Beirut, the MEF army could be caught now in a numeric disavantage, stretched thin along the Syrian coast.
On a High command meeting in 12 September 1936, the final plan for Operations ?? was decided. The advance was split into two advance fronts: the West and East. The Wast was the most important one and concentrated the highest amount of divisions: the whole II, III, IV and M Corps. The East advance would be responsability of the I Corps, with support of two Garrison divisions. 
The plan was as follows:
* I Corps, under Lieutenant General Halil Durmaz: advance towards Raqqa, and estabilish a defensive position along the upper side of the Euphrates River. The main objective of the advance was to confuse the French army of where the real offensive would be, and also, to deprive the French divisions in the Syrian desert of supplies.
* II Corps under Lieutenant General Gokberk Ates: secure and envelop Aleppo. The II Corps had no artillery support and would be relegated as supporting roles only.
* III Corps under Lieutenant General Korkut Guven: spearhead the main punch towards Antakya and Latakia, and quickly move south towards Beirut and Damascus to seize the port and block the French from bringing in reinforcements. The III Corps would also conduct the pincer around Aleppo.
* IV Corps under Lieutenant General Ulusoy Akkoz: conduct the attack to the east of Aleppo, and then close the pincer around the city, joining with the III Corps. After the envelope, the IV Corps would protect the supply lines to the south towards Beirut, taking and securing Homs.
* M Corps under Lieutenant General Mevlit Arman: the Mobile Corps would join the III Corps in the advance, and use the speed advantage to lead the way to Lebanon.
The West offensive would also not count with air support, as the MEF only had low range planes, and didn't had any airfield at the time that could assist the west region properly. The planes would then support the East advance instead, towards Raqqa.
The preparations and training for the west offensive were conducted in secret in the Antalya region, were the terrain was similar to the one the army would face in west Syria, while the east offensive trained near Konya. By late October, the MEF was ready for what was called Operation ?? (come up with a name here).
Iodinm received multiple reports of France mobilizing colonial trops in Africa and Asia, and Orbay and Toydemir warned him that each passing day, the chance of MEF winning the conflict was lower. They argued the Federation needed to take the gamble while their numbers were supperior. On 16 November, Iodinm gave in and the Council approved the operation.

## Attack on the Levant
### Initial attack
After formal war declaration at noon, the Operation was launched at 3pm of 16 November 1936. The attack was launched simultaniously. The MEF's III and M Corps attacked the Antakya region pushing towards Latakia, supported by 2 divisions of the II Corps, totalling 10 divisions (3 being Mountaineers) with almost 100 artillery pieces and 120 light tanks, mainly the soviet T-26. The French 3rs Motoirzed Infantry stationed in the region was caught by surprise overwelmed by the numbers, and on the same day retreated to the south of Aleppo. 7 more divisions assaulted the French positions east of Aleppo: the II Corps 8th, 9th and 12th Infantries, the IV Corps 17h, 18th, 19th, and 20th infantries. These units didn't had any artillery support but relied in the quick speed and numbers to overhelm the French 1st Motorized Infantry. East of the Euphrates, the remaning of the IV Corps, the 15th and 16th Infantry Divisions had the most difficult task: assault the French Sousse and 4th Armored divisions, without any artillery support, but with use of air support. The objective was not to drive them away, but to pin them down and prevent those divisions from crossing the Euphrates and supporting the 3rd Motorized Infantry. Finally, the I Corps launched the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd divisions agains the French 1st Cavalry division towards Raqqa, with the remaning 70 artillery pieces and air support.
Caught by surprise and faced with overwelming numbers, the French were unable to react or launch any form of counteattack. By 18 November 1936, the first elements of the M Corps reached the city and port of Latakia and took hold of the city, and kept pushing south towards Lebanon.

### Aleppo surrounded
The French transfered 120 tactical bombers to Damascus at the start of the war, and they started opeartions at 19 November. These planes proved to be a big hassle for the MEF, as their planes didn't have the reach to combat them, and the land units lacked any anti air capabilities. That was not enough to stop their advance though, and by 18 November the M Corps was already bordering Lebanon. That's when Giroud arrived in Beirut with his reinforcement army, and the 1st Colonial Infantry Division gave combat to the M Corps in an attempt to delay the advance long enough for the whole army to disembark. By 20 November, this sole French division was fighting 6 MEF divisions, at the same time that the MEF 14th Infantry took hold of Homs.
In 25 November the MEF 15th and 16th Divisions took Ayn al-Arab, and in 26 November, MEF 1st, 2nd, and 3rd took Raqqa in the Battle of Raqqa, and were receptioned warmly by the local population. By that point, the whole French resistance in the Raqqa region collapsed. The French army only holded Hasaka and Deir az-Zur, but was logistically defeated.
Meanhile, French 24th Infantry inside Aleppo was wary of leaving the city as they still waited for French's army reaction and reinforcements, and Division General Bugeaud knew the strategic importance of keeping the city. This decision proved costly though just a few days later, in the night of 28 November, when the II and IV Corps won the resistance of the battered French 1st and 3rd Motorized, and encircled Aleppo. The first phase of the Operation ?? was completed.

### Battle of Beirut
Elements of the M and III Corps were in battle with the Giraud army since 19 November. The MEF high command knew taking the port of Beirut was crucial to block the French from resupplying and reinforcing the army. They knew the MEF would not win the war if France was able to bring their war machine inside Syria.
The M and III Corps reached Beirut in the early hours of November 22, almost at the same time that the MEF 14th Infantry reached the outskirts of Damascus. Both Damascus and Beirut were already heavy protected with Giraud army, that was still unloading divisions. Almost 60,000 French soldier were stationed in Beirut and Damascus by that time, and more were comming. Both Guven and Arman, commanders of the III and M Corps, focused their efforts in hindering the disembark, while figting for the control of the city. The city was never under French control, and by 23 November further disembarkment of troops was not possible without risk of massive losses. By that point, the conflict was basically decided by the result of the Battle of Beirut, and both side sank many resources in winning the conflict.
By 3 December, the MEF had already reorganized their troops towards their efforts in Lebanon. While the I Corps would hold their positions in Raqqa and the Federation border, and the II Corps would keep Aleppo encircled and hold any attempts to release it, the IV Corps was assigned to the Lebanon region to help with the offensive. The MEF could not retreat for Beirut, or the French would resume their disembarkment of troops. The French still has daily bombardments in the city though, causing great losses. To remedy this, the MEF conducted an assault towards Damascus' airfield, which was taken in 4 December. That allowed the MEF to rebase their planes there and use them to intercept French bombers, while using their own as close air support.
When 1937 started, the battle was still going, and both sides had a lot of trouble supplying their units. The MEF had a thin and difficult supply line around Aleppo and using the port of Latakia, and the French were supplied only towards Beirut and improvised ports to the south, but both faced the danger of enemy submarines in the Meditarran.

### The fall of Aleppo
In the night of the 1 January 1937, the High Command of the MEF met in Latakia. It was clear that the path to win the war was to take Beirut and suffocate the rest of the French army. They also knew they could not stop the advance in the city as so to not allow any reiforcements to disembark, and to keep the assault going, the MEF had 10 divisions taking a couple of day turns in the fighting. However, the supply was very short and it was clear the assaulting forces would not have the means to take the city.
Günaltay suggested that Aleppo was assaulted and taken, which would greatly facilite the transport of supplies and allow the use of the railroads. The MEF initially avoided the idea of another urban battle to not create another Beirut, and not cause great civilian losts which would put the public opinion agains the MEF. But Günaltay argued that the Fench 24th Infantry stationed in the city would not last long if assaulted from all sides. They had negotiated the 24th Infantry surrend many times, but Bugeaud still had hopes of being relieved, as René Olry's army made constant attempts to liberate the city. The attack was scheduled for the early morning of the 4 January, and would be conducted by the II Corps, that was already enveloping the city. As Günaltay anticipated, Bugeaud's unit was not really fit for fight being more than 1 month trapped, and even with the eventual help of the Sousse Troop, they surrended on 7 January.
With Aleppo taken and the supply line reestructured, the MEF prepared for the last blow.
The French were now split into two sectors, completely cut off of each other. The Beirut/Damascus sector, under General Henri Giraud . And the Deir az-Zur sector, under General René Olry. Each had no more than 50,000 able soldiers, and dwindling supplies. René Olry position was specially bad, as they were mostly in a desert and some units were suffering from hunger and disease, and they didn't receive the eventual supply ship that Giraud had access to. Olry knew his units could not afford any offensive maneuvers to link both armies, so he made no attempt to do so. It was then, all down to Giraud.
Giraud's units where better off than Olry's ones, but they had a much harder task: they were holding both Beirut and Damascus, and were the main target of the MEF army. Giraud made several attempts to break off from his pocket, and on 11 Jnauray, he launched the last main French assault. He launched a two way offensive, in an attempt to disrupt the Beirut assault and free up the city to allow French reinforcements, and another towards Damascus air field, in an attempt to end MEF's air superiority and link up with Olry's forces. Despite some initial progress, the assault was called off in 16 Januray, when MEF reinforcements counter attacked.

### The end of the Battle of Beirut
After more than two months of combat, Beirut was taken by MEF forces in 31 January. Giraud knew the campaign in the Levant was over. He organized the defense of the beaches of south Lebanon and the city of Damascus, and requested Gamelin a retreat option. The French initially requested Britain for access to their Mandates so that their troops could be transported away. However, Britain feared that this could push the MEF into a war with their own Mandates, and denied the request.
General Olry decided to abandon Hasaka and the city of Deir el-Zur, concentrating his whole army in the desert, ready to flee towards Iraq, which was also denied.
With no other options, France tried to setup a improvised naval port to retreat Giraud's army by sea. The MEF air strikes and submarines in the area however caused great trouble to their effort, and extraction was slow.
On 13 February, the MEF launched Operation Capsize. The III, IV and M Corps assaulted the Lebanon beaches and the city of Damascus, while the I Corps advanced towards Olry positions in the Syrian desert. General Olry surrended in the same day, and many prisioners were taken. Giraud's units held a bit longer, when Damascus fell in 27 February, and the last units at the coast, in 5 March. Many French soldiers from both Giraud and Olry army fled to Iraq and Palestina even without the formal concent granted, but even then almost 50,000 French were made prisioners.
It was the end of the conflict and of French Mandate in the Levant.

## Aftermath
### Political
With the end of the conflict, French mandate ended in Syria and Lebanon. Both were susequently made part of the Middle Eastern Federation (Syria in 25 March 1937, Lebanon in 4 April 1937), and held seats in the Council, Syria with 4 seats, Lebanon with 3. Their administraive structure were integrated with the Federation and they were part of the MEF army draft by May 1937. The Levant population was largely in favour of the conflict, but Beirut citizens suffered heavily, and their religion differences to the rest of the Federation made them less satisfied with the outcome. Iodinm recognized this and articulated with the rest of the Council a joint support for Beirut's reconctruction, in the basis that any other state inside the Federation would receive the same support.
5 March is a Federal holiday in the Federation as the Levant Liberation date. 
### France
The conflict was a big blow to France's army pride. Once recognized as the best army in the world, the defeat by what was considered a lesser nation, and with so heavy losses, had grave repercutions around the world, and was one of the factors that embolded Hitler to enter a revenge war against France.
In combat though, the French army proved to be very competent. The MEF had numeric superiority through the whole conflict, and had the surprise factor on its favour. The biggest mistake was the slow mobilization: the MEF was mobilizing for a war since March, while France took long to field its army. If the whole colonial force were deployed to Syria, the MEF would not stand a chance and would not have won the war. Some strategic decisions were also questioned. René Olry prepared it's army for a counter-attack towards Malatya and Diyarbakir, leaving the west side fragile and exposed to a concentrated assault. Olry however didn't believe the MEF would setup such an agressive operation and reach Beirut before the whole of Giraud's army arrived.
### MEF
Iodinm and the Council member were very pleased with the MEF Army performance. Main strategist minds around the assault, Toydemir and Günaltay were specially praised, and the relatively freedom that the Corps and Division commanders had to lead towards each objective was proved very sucessful. However the MEF High Command was well aware that the win was only possible because the timing was correct. Had it been delayed by only a week more, the MEF would not have had success in seizing the coast and would end up in an attrition war with a much more powerful enemy. They also were very aware the MEF had superiority in number of soldier and artillery pieces, with undermined the French army reaction.
The war also exposed the inadequate equipment the MEF still had. The infantry weapons were less efective than French counterparts, their air force didn't had bombers, their fighter and CAS had a very short range and were of limited use, and their tanks were not a match for some of the tanks the French fielded, like the Hotchkiss H35. The MEF army was also very poorly motorized, with mules and horses still a standard. They also didn't had proper medical care units or engineers. In the end, they knew the victory was mostly because of good planning, and the usage of speraheads and concentrated attacks like the German advisors propagated.
