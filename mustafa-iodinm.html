                  Mustafa Iodinm (born 1892 in Izmir) is the founder of Iodinmism and Head of State of the Middle Eastern Federation since its establishment in 1923. As chairman of the Federal Council and holder of supreme command, he has presided over the construction of the federal army, the industrial program and the open claim to the mandated Levant.

                  The son of an Arab merchant family in the cosmopolitan port of Izmir, he served as a junior infantry officer on the Palestine and Mesopotamia fronts in the World War. During the Turkish Civil War of 1919–1923 he emerged as a third leader beside the Sultanate government and the Kemalist national movement, organizing a rally and newspaper network that demanded a federation of self-governing states under one army. After the compact of 1922 with Kurdish leaders and the victories of 1923, he chaired the constituent committees and assumed the headship of the new state.

                  In office he has combined sweeping powers of decree and initiative with government through the Council and the professional chiefs Marshal Kazim Orbay and Mehmet Günaltay. Federal publications present him as the mediator between Turks, Kurds and Arabs and the author of national survival; French official reporting presents him as an expansionist whose published program makes any accommodation over Syria impossible. He remains in office by sustained consensus, the Council retaining the constitutional power to remove him.

                  Early life

                  Family and childhood

                  Iodinm was born in 1892 in Izmir, then the great Levantine commercial city of the Aegean, to an Arab merchant household engaged in the grain and textile trade with the interior. Arabic was spoken at home, Turkish in school and the street, with Greek, Armenian and Ladino audible in the market. Federal hagiography dwells on the counting-house as the origin of his later federal arithmetic — autonomy priced in revenue, recruits and order — and on the port as proof that different peoples could live by one tariff. Of his parents and siblings the federal press publishes only that they were respectable merchants; no family papers have been released.

                  Education

                  He attended the local Turkish primary and junior secondary schools in Izmir with Arabic tutoring at home, a mediocre but diligent pupil by the few surviving registers. He did not pass through the full imperial staff course. What secondary education he possessed was interrupted by mobilization. He later acquired a working command French, sufficient to read manuals and to converse with the German mission, and a smattering of Kurmanji picked up on tour. The absence of a staff-college pedigree — in sharp contrast to Orbay, Toydemir and Solok — is the standard explanation for his method of command: he sets political objectives, arbitrates between services, and leaves technique to professionals.

                  War service

                  Conscripted during the general mobilization, he served in infantry regiments posted successively to Syria-Palestine and Mesopotamia. Like hundreds of thousands of junior officers he experienced the collapse of Ottoman supply — shoeless marches, requisitioned grain, typhus wards — the employment of Arab formations of doubtful loyalty, and the first sight of British and French colonial troops with motor transport and wireless. He ended the war in junior commissioned rank, probably lieutenant, and was demobilized with the armistice. He returned to a Smyrna under Greek occupation convinced, by his own later account, that dynastic government had forfeited any right to obedience and that heroism without organization would merely furnish a second and final partition.

                  Rise to power

                  Agitator and journalist

                  From early 1919 Iodinm organized demobilized soldiers into veterans associations that doubled as rally audiences and newspaper subscribers. His circuit — Izmir and its hinterland, the Ankara approaches, Adana, then Diyarbakir — combined open-air oratory with cheap pamphlets. Surviving fragments show a relentlessly concrete style: prices, requisitions, desertion figures, railway tonnages, rather than theology. His three standing points were that the Sultanate had sold the army in the war; that an exclusively Turkish republic, however heroic, would remain diplomatically friendless, dependent on foreign loans and munitions, and therefore indefensible once the powers recovered; and that only a written compact guaranteeing language, religion, local law and local governors could persuade distinct peoples to pay for and serve in one army. Merchant committees and junior officers funded the press; Kurdish notables attended from 1921.

                  Break with the old camps

                  Iodinm never belonged organizationally to either rival camp. He declined service under the Sultanate administration and refused Kemalist discipline, denouncing both in print — the Sultan as a broker of occupation, the Kemalists as brave men preparing a beautiful suicide. Neither camp could ignore him: the Sultanists because his rallies stripped garrisons of recruits, the Kemalists because his newspapers reached their own junior officers. Attempts to co-opt him as a propagandist failed on the autonomy condition, which neither rival would concede in writing.

                  The Exposure and the compact

                  Through 1921–1922 his papers published captured orders, supply returns and officers testimony alleging falsified Kemalist communiqués, inflated victory claims and reprisals against dissenting villages and Kurdish communities. Federal textbooks collect this material as the Exposure and credit it with detaching a substantial fraction of the nationalist officer corps. Outside federal historiography the truth of individual charges remains contested, and foreign scholars treat the dossier as a mixture of genuine documents, rumor and forgery assembled to political effect — which does not diminish its contemporary impact.

                  The written agreement of 1922 with Kurdish chiefs was the political masterpiece. Against a signed guarantee of Kurdish language administration and schooling, religious endowments, local courts and locally chosen governors, the chiefs placed their mountain contingents under federal discipline and accepted Turkish as the language of command. The infusion of fresh formations, combined with accelerating Kemalist desertions, decided the summer campaign of 1923. The remaining Kemalist field forces disintegrated; their leadership perished in the assassinations that federal histories attribute to rival nationalists under “strange circumstances.” Sultanist garrisons surrendered town by town for want of any popular cause. No foreign army intervened on the ground.

                  Head of State

                  Consolidation of power

                  The constituent committees of late 1923–1924, which Iodinm chaired in person, wrote the compact that still governs. Removal requires ten of fourteen votes. The power has never been exercised. Ex-Sultanist notables were absorbed as governors and judges.

                  His method of rule is itinerant. He tours the states continuously. Ministers complain that decisions emerge from tour conversations rather than memoranda. Supporters reply that the tours are the government.

                  Economic policy

                  With the state leadership he imposed reconstruction first and rearmament second. The tariff of 1926 and the federal bank centralized customs while leaving direct taxation to the states. Steel and rifles at Kırıkkale, shell filling, railway repair at Ankara, textiles at Adana, cement at Istanbul and organized remount depots in the east were built with German, Soviet, Czech and American machinery indiscriminately purchased. Primary schooling expanded in three languages; technical and war schools were rebuilt. By 1935 the economy remained agrarian and animal-powered, but capable, in foreign commercial judgment, of equipping a regional army for a short campaign. Iodinm personally opened each major plant, invariably linking output figures to independence in the same speech.

                  Foreign and military policy

                  Iodinm proclaims the Levant, Iraq, Yemen and Armenian districts as naturally belonging to a future union, while maintaining legations and trade with every power. He has kept the small German mission since 1924 and widened it after 1933, when Berlin began to value Istanbul as a diversionary auxiliary against France and Britain and a possible participant against Russia; he has simultaneously bought Soviet armor, Czech designs and American second-hand aircraft. German inspections praise cohesion and marching power and deplore artillery, signals and motors; Iodinm quotes the praise in public and acts, partially, on the censure in the 1934–1936 programs. His published liberation program — restated in nearly every major address — is cited in Paris as proof that Syrian independence on mandate terms would merely deliver the Levant disarmed, and in Berlin as proof of reliability.

                  Personal life

                  Federal publications state that he is married with children and divide his residence between Istanbul and his tours; no names, portraits or memoirs of the family have been authorized. His recreations are listed as riding and the reading of military history. His oratory, preserved in newsreel fragments, is rapid, statistical and interrogative, working crowds through questions about prices and rations rather than through lyric flights. He neither drinks nor smokes in public. His health is officially excellent; foreign rumor-mongering to the contrary is periodically denounced.

                  Ideology

                  Federal publications define Iodinmism as the doctrine that self-government in the region is possible only under a united flag guaranteeing autonomy, condensed domestically as “One Flag, Many Peoples.” Its articles are: sovereignty from compact, not dynasty or blood; secular federal codes for commerce, customs, railways and war; cultural and religious self-government in the states; a customs union with a federal bank; trilingual schooling with Turkish command; and a common army funded by fixed quotas. The doctrine repudiates both legitimism and integral nationalism by name.

                  Supporters present it as an application of the early American federal example to Levantine conditions; French critics present it as a juridical cover for annexation; German admirers present it as the eastern counterpart of their own national rebirth; Soviet commentators present it as bourgeois nationalism with clerical trimmings. Iodinm himself, in the collected addresses, insists it is bookkeeping elevated to patriotism: peoples who will not pay for one another will not fight for one another, and peoples who will not fight for one another will be ruled by those who will.

                  Religious views

                  A practicing Muslim, Iodinm attends the principal feasts in Istanbul, Ankara and Van in turn and presents himself as protector of mosques, madrasas and endowments within the states, while upholding secular federal codes and refusing any religious test for federal office. The duality explains his following among ulema and guild networks and, equally, the standing reservations of rigorist groups such as the Arab-Islamic Force, which demand explicit Islamic references in any future Syrian constitution. He has declined to adjudicate theology, replying that the compact guarantees worship and leaves definition to the worshippers.

                  Views on minorities

                  He guarantees Kurdish language administration and schooling in Kurdistan, Arabic commerce and schooling in designated districts, and the protection of the remaining Greek, Armenian and Jewish congregations in Istanbul and Izmir. Turkish remains the language of command, promotion and higher administration, and autonomy is expressly conditional on the delivery of revenue, recruits, animals and order. Critics call the condition a leash; the official press calls it the definition of federal citizenship. Both agree it is enforced. Kurdish mountain troops, Arab guides and Turkish gunners parade together on constituent day as the living emblem.

                  Public image

                  In Turkey and Kurdistan he is commemorated as mediator of the 1922 compact and chairman of the constituent committees: portraits in federal uniform without marshal insignia, underlining rule as chairman rather than field commander; school dictations of rally passages; newsreels of arsenal openings and harvest inspections. The opposition abroad inverts the image: Paris shows an expansionist conspirator with German officers in the background; Berlin shows a reliable auxiliary; Moscow shows a clerical nationalist; London shows a watchful file marked Mosul and Palestine. No authorized biography independent of the federal press exists, and foreign correspondents are rarely granted interviews.
